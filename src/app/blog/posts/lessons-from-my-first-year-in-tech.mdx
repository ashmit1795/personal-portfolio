Let's get one thing out of the way: **this is not a technical post.**
 
No architecture diagrams. No "10 Node.js tips". No code blocks (okay, maybe one joke).
 
I've spent time at two very different places now. First as a Backend Developer Intern at [Nurdd](https://nurdd.club/), and for the last three months as an SWE Intern at [KFintech](https://kfintech.com/). Somewhere between the late-night debugging sessions and the "can we get on a quick call?" messages, I realised that the things that shaped me most weren't frameworks or databases.
 
They were *habits*. Small, boring, unglamorous habits.
 
College teaches you DSA, DBMS, OS and CN. Nobody teaches you how to message a senior manager, what to do when your teammate is blocking you, or why you should never trust a verbal "yeah, just change it".
 
So here's the cheat sheet I wish someone had handed me on day one. 🚀
 
---
 
## 1. Stay humble. Always.
 
You will work with people who've been writing code since before you knew what a `for` loop was. You'll also work with interns, juniors, and people from totally different teams who know things you don't.
 
Be humble with *all* of them.
 
Nobody likes the person who treats every conversation like a debate to win. The best engineers I've met are the ones who say "I might be wrong here, can you walk me through it?" without any ego attached.
 
Humility isn't weakness. It's the fastest way to learn.
 
---
 
## 2. Own your work. Honour your commitments.
 
Ownership is a big word, so let me make it concrete:
 
- If you say you'll do it by Thursday, it's done by Thursday.
- If something's going to slip, **you** say it first, early, and with a new date. Not on Thursday evening.
- Your task isn't "done" when the code is written. It's done when it works, it's tested, and the person depending on it knows about it.
And here's the flip side that took me a while to learn: **don't over-commit.**
 
When you're new, you want to impress. So you say "yes, sure, I can do that" to everything. Then you're juggling five things, delivering all of them late, and impressing exactly nobody.
 
A realistic "I can get this done by Friday" beats an ambitious "tomorrow!" that turns into next week. Under-promise, over-deliver. It's a cliché because it works.
 
---
 
## 3. Respect your seniors' time (don't just bump them)
 
This one's a classic rookie mistake, and I've done it too.
 
You hit a problem. A senior or manager could solve it in two minutes. So you ping them: *"Hi sir, are you free?"* or worse, you walk up and start talking.
 
But they might be in the middle of something important. A meeting, a deadline, a deep-focus task you're about to derail.
 
What works better:
 
1. **Ask for their availability.** "Do you have 15 minutes sometime today or tomorrow?"
2. **Set the context upfront.** One or two lines on what it's about.
3. **Be clear in your own head first.** Know what you're stuck on, what you've already tried, and what you need from them.
A well-framed question gets a quick answer. A vague "can you help me with something?" gets a long, confusing conversation for both of you. Showing up prepared tells people you value their time, and they'll value yours back.
 
---
 
## 4. Be the person others can count on
 
Be available to help your teammates and colleagues. If someone's stuck and you know the answer (or can at least point them in the right direction), take five minutes and help.
 
It costs you very little, and it builds something that no performance review can: **trust.**
 
Team culture isn't built in meetings. It's built in the small moments when someone says "hey, can you look at this?" and the answer is "sure, let me take a look."
 
And that goes both ways, which brings me to the next one.
 
---
 
## 5. Don't hesitate to ask for help. From *anyone*.
 
You don't have to wait for a senior to ask a question. A junior might know that specific module better than anyone else on the team. A peer might have hit this exact bug last week.
 
Hierarchy matters for decisions. It doesn't matter for *knowledge*.
 
Reaching out to someone at your level, or even below, isn't embarrassing. Spending three hours stuck on something a colleague could've explained in three minutes? *That's* the expensive option.
 
---
 
## 6. Dependencies: communicate, follow up, give deadlines
 
Real projects are full of dependencies. Your work waits on someone, and someone else's work waits on you.
 
**When you're blocked by someone:**
 
- Don't just sit there and watch your timeline slip silently.
- Tell your manager clearly: what you're waiting on, from whom, and how it's affecting your deadline.
- Follow up. Not once. Continuously, and politely, until it's cleared.
**When someone is blocked by you:**
 
- Treat it as a priority, because someone else's timeline is now tied to yours.
- Don't be vague. "I'll get to it soon" is not an answer. Give a **concrete deadline**, and if it changes, say so.
Silence is where projects go to die. A two-line status message can save a team a week of confusion.
 
---
 
## 7. Business logic needs a paper trail
 
This one is my favourite "learned the hard way" lesson (thankfully, the *easy* version of the hard way).
 
If you're writing code that implements **business rules**, such as calculations, eligibility conditions or anything that touches how the business actually operates, never rely on a verbal confirmation or a casual chat message.
 
Get it in writing from the **authorised person**. An email, a BRD, a formal sign-off. Something you can point to.
 
Why?
 
- Requirements get misremembered. By everyone. Including you.
- People change their minds, or leave the team, or forget what they said.
- If something breaks in production, "someone told me in a call" is not a great place to be standing.
It's not about blame or distrust. It's about making sure everyone is working from the same source of truth.
 
---
 
## 8. Document everything. Keep your manager in the loop.
 
Write things down. What you did, why you did it, what you decided, what's pending.
 
- Future-you will thank you when you've forgotten why a decision was made.
- Teammates will thank you when they pick up your work.
- Your manager will thank you because there are no surprises.
Speaking of which: **keep your manager in the loop.** Not in a "I'm reporting every sneeze" way, but enough that they always know what you're working on, where it stands, and what might go wrong. Nobody likes finding out about a problem from a third person.
 
---
 
## 9. Know the bigger picture
 
Whichever company you're in, whichever domain, **learn the domain.**
 
It's tempting to say "I just write the code, someone else handles the business side." But the engineers who grow fast are the ones who understand:
 
- What does this product actually do for the business?
- What's the high-level flow of the system, and where does my piece fit in?
- What happens upstream and downstream of my module?
Especially in a domain like fintech, where one wrong assumption can mean real money and real compliance headaches, this isn't optional. When you understand the *why*, your *how* gets so much better. You catch problems before they're bugs. You ask sharper questions. You stop being a task executor and start being an engineer.
 
---
 
## 10. Keep upskilling. The market doesn't owe you stability.
 
Having an internship or a job doesn't mean you can coast.
 
The tech world is volatile. Tools change. Roles evolve. Entire categories of work get reshaped by new technology faster than anyone expected. Job security is not really a thing anymore. **Adaptability is.**
 
So keep learning. Build things on the side. Read. Pick up the skill that's one step beyond your current role. Not out of panic, but out of curiosity and self-respect.
 
And when your company runs **upskilling workshops or training sessions, attend them.** Seriously. It's free learning, on company time, from people who know the internal context. Don't skip them just because you have a ticket to close.
 
---
 
## 11. Respect office hours (and your own life)
 
Controversial opinion, maybe: **don't make a habit of working after office hours.**
 
I know. We glorify the late-night grind. But here's the honest truth: the company has its own business priorities, and it will always put them first. That's not evil, it's just how it works. Your time and energy are *your* responsibility to protect.
 
Work hard and stay focused *during* working hours. Plan your day properly. Communicate early when something's heavy. And then log off.
 
Rest isn't laziness. A tired brain writes buggy code, and burnt-out engineers don't have much to give to anyone, including the company.
 
(Yes, there are occasional production fires where you stay late. That's different. I'm talking about making it your default.)
 
---
 
## 12. Don't gossip. Play it safe.
 
If I could only put one thing on this list in bold, it would be this one.
 
Offices are full of chatter, about people, about decisions, about who said what. You'll hear things. Some of it will be interesting, and some of it will be juicy.
 
**Keep it to yourself.**
 
- Don't talk about colleagues behind their backs.
- Don't pass on office affairs.
- If you learn something about someone, it stays with you.
Gossip travels faster than any network packet, and it always finds its way back to the source. Being known as someone who can be trusted with information, and who doesn't take part in the rumour mill, is a quiet superpower. Your reputation is built over months and destroyed in one casual conversation.
 
---
 
## 13. Keep personal and professional separate
 
Two worlds. Different rules.
 
- Don't carry your personal stress into your work conversations.
- Don't drag office drama into your personal life.
You're allowed to be a human being at work, of course. Be friendly, be warm, have a laugh at lunch. But having a clear boundary between the two keeps both healthier. You show up at work focused, and you go home actually *home*.
 
---
 
## The Real Takeaway
 
If I look at this whole list, almost none of it is technical.
 
It's about **communication**, **accountability**, **respect**, **trust** and **curiosity**. The tech industry is full of smart people. What makes someone someone you *want* on your team isn't just how well they code, it's how they show up.
 
I'm still very much learning. I've made mistakes at both places, and I'll make more. This list is as much a reminder to myself as it is advice for anyone reading.
 
If you're a student about to step into your first internship, or you're a few months in like me, I hope something here saves you a bit of trial and error.
 
And if you've got your own "wish I'd known this earlier" lessons, I'd genuinely love to hear them. 🙌
 
Until next time, keep building, keep learning, and **keep your commits clean.** 😄
